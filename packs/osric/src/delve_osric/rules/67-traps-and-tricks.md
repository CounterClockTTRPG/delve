title: Designing traps and tricks
source: gmg

**Danger grades.** Pitch traps to their place. Taking a hidden trapdoor as the example:

- **Nuisance:** a 10-ft drop.
- **Hazardous:** a 10-ft drop onto spikes.
- **Dangerous:** onto poisoned spikes, perhaps with the lid locking shut behind.
- **Fatal:** all of that, then a stone block shaped to the pit dropping in from above.

A first-level corridor leading to poor monsters merits a nuisance; a hoard guarded while its dragons hunt, on a deep level, merits fatal. Traps can be chained (a pit that sets off a swinging log). OSRIC's trap table is a starting point for variations, not a complete list.

**Traps in a living dungeon.** Intelligent creatures near a trap always have a way past it: another route, a mechanism, a password or spell. If the trap guards their lair, treasure or young, they maintain it, even cleaning away bloodstains. When the party learns to beat it, clever monsters may move it, add to it or replace it.

**Purpose.** Traps raise the stakes and reward careful play; a good dungeon mixes traps, monsters and encounters for talk. For finding and disarming, see searching and traps; early-level damage should stay low (around 1d6, 1d10 at most), and deadly traps should usually allow a save.

**Tricks** turn expectations upside down: harmless things that look deadly, deadly things that look harmless. A fine ring in a lead box turns out to be a worthless copy, but the "lead" box is painted gold. Build one by taking a common dungeon object and giving it an unexpected property (OSRIC has two tables for this). Tricks give players puzzles to think through rather than dice to roll.
